The Hand Release Push Up Standard Explained

The hand release push up is the event that looks simple and then humbles almost everyone who tries it with strict form. Each repetition starts with your chest on the ground, ends with your arms fully extended, and includes a clear lift of both hands off the surface at the bottom. That pause and release remove the bounce and the sagging half reps that people use in regular push ups. If you want a high score on this event, you need to train the exact movement, not just general pressing strength.

Begin with the setup. Lie face down with your chest, hips and thighs touching the ground. Place your hands flat beside your chest, roughly shoulder width or slightly wider, with fingers pointing forward. Your feet can be together or a short distance apart, but your toes are the only part of your legs that should carry weight once you are up. Your body should form a straight line from the back of your head to your heels.

On the way up, press the ground away and keep your hips and chest rising together. A common fault is lifting the chest first while the hips stay down, which looks like a worm and can be called as a no rep. Keep your abs tight and squeeze your glutes. At the top, your elbows must be fully locked out before you lower yourself again. Many soldiers lose repetitions because they stop an inch short of lockout when they get tired.

On the way down, lower under control until your chest and thighs touch the ground. You do not need to rest there, but you must complete the touch. Then lift both hands clear of the surface for a brief moment, extend your arms out to the sides or simply raise your palms, and place them back under your shoulders. This release is the signature of the event. Practice it slowly at first so that it becomes a clean habit instead of a rushed flick.

Pacing matters here just as much as it does in the run. Try to find a rhythm you can hold for the full two minutes, such as one repetition every two or three seconds. Starting with a burst of fast reps usually ends in a long rest at the top and a drop in your total count. Short rests in the plank position are allowed, but only if your body stays straight, so use them wisely and do not let your hips sag while you breathe.

For training, build volume in several ways. Do sets of five to eight perfect hand release reps with a full release each time, rest for a minute, and repeat for four to six rounds. Add a day of slow tempo reps where you take three seconds going down. Add a day of submaximal ladders, such as one rep, two reps, three reps and so on up to your limit and back down. Finish with plank holds and scapular push ups to strengthen the shoulders and the muscles around the shoulder blades.

Support work helps too. Triceps dips, overhead presses and rows keep the shoulders balanced and reduce the risk of pain. Core training such as dead bugs, hollow holds and side planks supports your straight body line. Mobility work for the wrists, including gentle circles and forearm stretches, protects you from the discomfort that can appear when you increase volume. Take a rest day between hard push up sessions so your joints recover.

Track your results every week. Count your reps in a two minute test, note how many came from the first minute and how many from the second, and record any reps you think would have been called as no reps. Over time you will see whether you are fading early or whether you could start faster. When you have a new number, you can check a free AFT score calculator to see how many points that raw score earns and how it compares with your other events. That helps you decide whether the next block of training should go to push ups or to something else.

Finally, remember that the best way to practice standards is with a partner. Ask a friend to watch your chest touches, your lockout and your hand release, and to call out the reps that would not count. Honest feedback makes your practice reps match what a grader will see on test day. Stay patient, keep your form tight, and your numbers will rise steadily over a couple of months of consistent work.
