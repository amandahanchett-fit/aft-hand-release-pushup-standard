The Hand Release Push Up Standard on the Army Fitness Test

The hand release push up is the upper body event that opens the Army Fitness Test, and it is also the event where small form mistakes cost the most repetitions. This page explains what the standard asks for, how graders count, and how to practice so that every repetition you do counts on test day.

Starting position

You begin in the front leaning rest. Your hands sit on the ground about shoulder width apart, fingers pointing forward, with your arms fully extended. Your feet may be together or up to a boot width apart. Your body forms a straight line from the back of your head to your heels. Your hips should not sag toward the floor and should not pike up toward the ceiling.

The repetition

On the way down, you lower your whole body as one unit until your chest touches the ground. Do not rest on the floor and do not let your belly or hips land first. Once your chest makes contact, you lift both hands completely off the surface, extending your arms out to the sides in a T shape. That brief release is what separates this movement from a standard push up, and it removes the bounce and the stretch reflex that can make repetitions easier.

After the hands return to the ground, you press up until your arms are fully locked out and your body is again a straight plank. A repetition only counts when you reach full extension with a straight back and legs. If you stop short of lockout, the grader will not credit that repetition.

Rest and scoring

You have two minutes to complete as many valid repetitions as you can. You may rest only in the up position with your arms locked, and you may not rest with your knees or belly on the ground. If any part of your body other than your hands and feet touches the ground, or if you rotate or lift one hand at a time, the event ends for that attempt. Your total valid repetitions convert to a score that depends on your age group and sex under the Army Fitness Test scoring tables.

Common mistakes to fix

The most frequent fault is a sagging midsection. Squeeze your glutes and brace your abdominal muscles the entire time. The second fault is a partial range of motion, where the chest hovers above the ground. Practice touching your chest to the ground every time. The third fault is rushing the release, so your hands only hover for a moment and barely leave the surface. Make the release deliberate and visible to the grader.

How to train

Practice the full movement two or three days each week. Do sets at around seventy percent of your best two minute total, keep the rest short, and film yourself from the side so you can see your hips. Add scapular push ups, planks, and slow lowering sets to build the control you need. Pace yourself on test day, because starting too fast leads to forced rests during the final minute.

Turn repetitions into points

Knowing your repetition target helps you plan training. If you serve or want to serve in a physically demanding specialty, check the standards that apply to your role. For example, the AFT Calculator shows the Army Fitness Test requirements and score conversions for the 18C Special Forces Engineer job, so you can see how many hand release push ups you need alongside the other four events.

Treat the standard as a skill. Learn the rules, rehearse them with a partner who grades you strictly, and your two minute total will rise steadily.
